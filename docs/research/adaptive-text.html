Problem: You asked whether a textbook written for everyone could teach you in a tenth of the pages, at the right level of challenge, with the ideas that matter brought forward. Done is what research has measured, ranked into designs for a selected passage, with a demo of the best-supported one. 1 · The words this page uses

What you already know predicts where you end up, not how much a given text adds, and your own sense of it is a weak guide

Correlation r runs from −1 to 1; 0 means no relation. Sources: Simonsmeier 2022 (a meta-analysis of 8,776 effect sizes, checked); Zell & Krizan 2014 (22 meta-analyses, abstract); Dunlosky & Lipko 2007 (from a summary; the abstract says "quite poor" without a number).

  • Prior knowledge: what you know about the topic before reading. It strongly predicts your final score (r = .53) but, on average, not your gain (r = −.06); the gain swings widely from study to study, so the same text is efficient for some readers and wasteful for others.
    For the design: how much you know about a topic overall does not say which chunk you can skip, so the test has to be a question about each chunk.
  • Self-rating: your own sense of how much you know. People's ratings of their own ability track their performance weakly (r ≈ .29, Zell & Krizan), and the gap is largest for explanations of how things work (Rozenblit & Keil 2002), which is most of what textbooks teach.
  • Prequestion: a question asked before you read, which you answer or guess. It doubles as a quick test of what you know.
  • Idea unit: a chunk of a passage that carries one claim. Gist: that claim in one line. Test out: answer the unit's questions right, and you get the gist instead of the chunk. Compression: testing out applied to a whole passage, rank 2 below; the decision is about this.
  • Refutation text: a text that states a common wrong belief, says why it is wrong, then explains the right account.
  • Expertise reversal: support that helps a beginner (definitions, worked examples, restatement, highlighting) stops helping, or gets in the way, once you know the topic.
  • Effect size (g or d): the gain in standard deviations, as on the companion pages: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large; SD (standard deviation) is the same unit. Randomised: groups assigned by chance. Quasi-experimental: groups compared, but not assigned by chance.

2 · What is measured

Cutting works when the main ideas stay and a test decides the cut; questions before reading help only what they ask about

Hatched bars: figure from a summary, not checked against the paper. The faint bar is the cost: prequestions do nothing for what they don't ask.

Shorter text that keeps the main ideas

Readers given short summaries of textbook chapters matched or beat readers of the full chapters, on facts and on inferences, tested up to 6–12 months later (Reder & Anderson 1980).

Those summaries were written by people and studied as the text itself, and a follow-up found part of the advantage came from meeting the main points again (Reder & Anderson 1982). Separately, AI summaries often overstate scientific conclusions (Peters & Chin-Yee 2025), and letting an AI do the work, by chatting with it instead of taking notes, lowered learning three days later (Kreijkes 2025).

Cutting what a test shows you know

In "curriculum compacting", teachers pretested 336 able pupils and cut what they already knew, by as much as 40–50% for some; on achievement tests they scored as well as pupils taught everything (Reis et al. 1998, randomised by school district).

The tests were near their ceiling (median scores above the 90th percentile), so a small loss could hide.

This is the closest real-world version of "test out, then compress".

The evidence in detail
  • Questions before reading: preregistered meta-analysis, St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024: g = 0.54 on the asked content (97 effects), 0.04 on other content (91 effects; 46% of studies showed no effect or a negative one). The effect holds even when the first answers are mostly wrong (Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009; Pan & Carpenter 2023). Checked in the full text.
  • Expertise reversal: named by Kalyuga et al. 2003. A 2025 meta-analysis of 60 studies (Tetzlaff et al.) reports high support beating low support for beginners (d ≈ 0.51) and the reverse for knowledgeable learners (d ≈ 0.43), largest in higher education; checked against the abstract. Signalling the links between text and pictures helps mainly beginners (Richter et al. 2016, r = .17).
  • Making an expert fill gaps: removing the connecting words so knowledgeable readers infer more helped in McNamara et al. 1996, but later only for weaker readers (O'Reilly & McNamara 2007) and not at all at n = 511 (2020 preprint, summary only). So cut content you know, not the links between ideas.
  • Correcting a confident wrong belief: refutation texts (state it, refute it, explain) g = 0.41, 44 comparisons (Schroeder & Kucera 2022, abstract).
  • Interesting but irrelevant detail lowers learning, g ≈ −0.33 (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, figure from summaries).
  • AI simplification at scale: a randomised study of 4,563 readers found AI-simplified texts raised multiple-choice comprehension by 3.9 points (Guidroz et al. 2025, Google, abstract checked); the system was tuned with an automatic check for lost information, but the reader study did not test for loss, nor test readers who knew the topic.
  • AI summaries drop qualifiers: 4,900 summaries from 10 models frequently overgeneralised scientific conclusions (Peters & Chin-Yee 2025, abstract).

3 · Challenge and insight

"The right level of challenge" and "aha" have good theory but thin numbers; the best-evidenced part is working the idea out yourself

ClaimEvidenceVerdict
Learning is fastest at about 85% correctWilson et al. 2019: simulated learners on yes/no tasks; no human experimentuntested in people
You learn most just beyond what you knowMetcalfe & Kornell 2005, eight experiments: people choose that material when they control studysupported as a pattern
Curiosity peaks when you almost knowKang 2009 (n = 19 in its scanner experiment, trivia), Wade & Kidd 2019; not tested on textbook prosesmall samples
Harder-to-read text helpsXie 2018: hard fonts, d ≈ 0 across 25 articles (summary)no
Working it out beats reading itGeneration effect, 86 studies, ≈ 0.40 SD (Bertsch 2007; mostly word lists, not prose)yes
An "aha" makes it stickDanek 2013 (magic tricks): 64% of aha solutions recalled after 14 days against 52%; Kizilirmak 2016: generating and the aha feeling each relate to memory, independentlyyes, in puzzles

So "challenge" for a reading tool means harder to recall, not harder to read. And the way to serve natural insights is to stop before the sentence that gives the key idea away and ask you to predict it. "Ideas that matter" has no reliable way to be identified: the main framework for it (threshold concepts) has no agreed definition, so the app should pick the central claim and let your answer show whether it mattered to you.

5 · Try it

Answer first, and the chunks you get right shrink to one line; the central idea always plays in full

The same eye-movement facts as the companion pages, written as a textbook passage. Answer each question before its chunk appears; the figures are from Rayner 1998, as on Reading methods.

1 · While your eyes cross a line of text, they…

Gist: the eyes jump between stops, about 30 ms per jump, and read nothing while moving.

When we read, our eyes do not glide smoothly across the line. They move in quick jumps, called saccades, from one stopping point to the next. Each jump takes about 30 milliseconds, and almost nothing is taken in while the eye is moving.

2 · How long does the eye rest on a typical stop?

Gist: each stop lasts about a quarter of a second; jumps of 7–9 letters skip many short words.

Between jumps the eye rests on a word for about a quarter of a second, a pause called a fixation. A typical jump carries it seven to nine letters forward, so short words are often skipped and recognised from the edge of vision.

3 · How often do the eyes jump backwards?

Gist: 10–15% of jumps go back, usually to a word passed too quickly.

About 10 to 15 percent of jumps go backwards, usually to a word that was passed too quickly or misread. Skilled readers make them too; blocking them lowers comprehension.

★ 4 · Central idea, so it is never cut. Predict: where does most reading time go? (uses your numbers above)

Add it up: each stop takes about 250 ms and each jump about 30 ms, so the stops are 250 ÷ 280 ≈ 89% of the time. Reading speed is limited by how fast you recognise words, not by how fast your eyes move. That is why tricks that only cut eye movement save little.

A wrong or "not sure" answer always shows the chunk in full. A gist can always be opened. In the app the questions would be written by the model, two per chunk; here they are hand-written, one per chunk.

8 · Decision

One choice, after Beyond reading's: whether the learning key also cuts the chunks you show you know

Depends on · Beyond reading, first decision: a learning key at all (recommended yes)

Should the learning key also compress the passage?

Questions alone (rank 1) need the model only to write questions: on Beyond reading it wrote 13 of 15 usable ones over five passages (no saved log), and a rerun here wrote three usable questions in 9.6 s. Compression also needs it to write options with a correct key and to pick the ★ chunk, neither tested yet. Your answer is checked by code against the key, so no model grades you; the risks are a wrong key and a lucky guess (section 7).

Like the voice, the model is part of macOS, not a third-party library; but unlike the rest of the app you cannot read inside it, so its output is checked rather than understood.

All thresholds are my estimates, not from a study: one wrong key in 30 would hide a chunk you didn't know about roughly once in ten passages. One reader is a noisy test.

Not verified, and gaps in the literature
  • Figures from summaries, not checked against the papers: seductive details −0.33; Dunlosky & Lipko r ≈ .27; Salden's d = .59 (in the full report); the 2020 preprint with n = 511. Checked by the fact judge and now untagged: Tetzlaff 2025, the generation effect 0.40, Kalyuga & Sweller r up to .92, VanLehn 0.79 / 0.76, and the model's 4,096-token context.
  • The demo's eye-movement figures match secondary sources citing Rayner 1998; Rayner's text was not opened.
  • Some DOIs in the full report were filled in from memory and not opened.
  • No randomised study has an AI model adapt a text to a reader's measured knowledge and then test learning. Google's "Learn Your Way" (n = 60) cannot separate its parts; Huynh & McNamara 2025 measured text features only.
  • Open: how many questions are enough to gate a chunk; whether curiosity's peak holds for textbook prose; whether an "aha" from text carries the memory benefit; whether pacing the one-word-at-a-time reader by what is new to you helps.
  • Tutoring's claimed gain of two standard deviations, "2 sigma" (Bloom 1984), does not hold up: of 96 tutoring studies in a 2020 review, none showed it, and about half the original effect came from frequent tests with correction (von Hippel 2024). Frequent checks are what a reading tool can supply.
