Reading for Nerds · research · Nov 7 · next: Beyond reading, Adaptive text

No display in the studies makes reading much faster without costing comprehension: RSVP tops out near normal speed, and the best-backed gain is listening at up to 2×, roughly 20% above silent reading

Problem: We built an RSVP reader, and you want to know what else people have tried for faster screen reading, and which methods hold up in studies. Done is the methods ranked by measured outcomes, each with a small demo and its evidence, and what that means for our app. It needs two choices from you, at the end.

1 · The words this page uses

Reading time goes into understanding words, not moving the eyes, so cutting eye movement saves little

Fixations: stopped on a word, about 250 ms each
Jumps

Share of reading time, from the review by Rayner and colleagues (2016). Spritz, the 2014 app that made RSVP with a red pivot letter popular, claimed eye movement takes 80% of reading time; the review reports that claim and disputes it (figures in this review not rechecked against its full text).

These terms carry the rest of the page.

  • Fixation: the eye stops on a word, about a quarter of a second; this is where reading happens.
  • Saccade: the jump to the next stop, 20 to 35 ms, about 7 letters.
  • Regression: a jump back; 10 to 15% of jumps, mostly to repair a misunderstanding.
  • Preview: the eye already takes in about 14 letters to the right of where it rests, which is why about 30% of words are skipped.
  • wpm: words per minute. A meta-analysis of 190 studies puts adult silent reading at 238 wpm for non-fiction and 260 for fiction.
  • Meta-analysis: a study that pools many studies into one estimate. n: the number of people tested.
  • Effect size (g): a difference in standard deviations, so studies can be compared: 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. g = 0.41 means the average reader in one group beat about two thirds of the other.
  • Not significant (or a large p, such as p = 0.9): the difference is small enough to be chance.

The review's conclusion follows: what limits reading speed is how fast we recognise words and build meaning, not eye control, and speed trades against understanding.

What this means for our app: if eye travel is about 10% of reading time, removing it saves at most about 10%, and RSVP also shows every word, including the ones the eye would have skipped. So RSVP can match normal reading but should not beat it, and the studies in method 3 agree. What it gives instead is reading with no eye travel, in a small window, at a pace set for you, which is also what the voice needs. It doesn't change what we build; it changes what to expect: the app will not make you a faster reader, but it can make reading easier to start and keep going.

3 · Each method, with a demo

Each demo runs on the same passage, so you can feel the difference

1 Listening, or reading while listening, at up to 2× Small gain

voice speed 1.5×

Uses your browser's voice; each word lights up as it is spoken, like our app's V mode.

How it works: speech carries the words, so the eyes are freed or paced; synced text lets you follow along.

  • Speed costs little up to 2×. Over 200 students watched a 15-minute lecture at 1×, 1.5×, 2× or 2.5×: little loss up to 2×, a drop at 2.5×, tested immediately and a week later. One lecture, not dense text. Murphy et al. 2022
  • Reading while listening helps when the pace is set for you. Meta-analysis of 30 studies, n = 1,945: overall g = 0.18; when the experimenter set the pace g = 0.41; self-paced g = 0.06, not significant. Clinton-Lisell 2023
  • Listening and reading give about the same comprehension overall (46 studies, n = 4,687, g = 0.07, not significant), but reading beats listening on inference questions (g = 0.36) and when the reader sets the pace (g = 0.13). Clinton-Lisell 2022, Review of Educational Research
  • The working for "roughly 20%": speech at 1× is about 150 wpm, so 2× is about 300 wpm (Rayner et al. 2016, not rechecked), against 238–260 wpm silent; 300 / 250 ≈ 1.2. No single study compared 2× listening with silent reading: this joins Murphy's lecture result with the reading-speed meta-analysis.
Pros
The best-supported way to go above normal speed without losing comprehension. Our app already has it.
Cons
Caps out near 2× speech, about 300 wpm. Loses to reading on inference questions, so it suits gist more than study. No study tested RSVP plus a synced voice specifically.

In our app: in V mode the voice sets the pace and the wpm setting is not used; ↑ ↓ change the voice's rate, and the footer shows the speed it reaches. V mode is the "pace set for you" case where reading while listening helped most (g = 0.41); that it carries over to RSVP is my inference, not a tested result.

2 Practice while watching your own speed Small gain

How it works: reading regularly while tracking your speed and checking your understanding; the gain comes from fewer, shorter fixations, not a new eye skill.

  • 30 German undergraduates: a commercial speed-reading app, a self-monitoring training, or nothing. Both trained groups gained about 35 wpm with no comprehension change. Klimovich, Tiffin-Richards & Richter 2023 (randomisation not verified)

No demo: this is a habit, not a display.

Pros
Real gain with comprehension held; costs nothing.
Cons
One small study; about +15%, not multiples.

3 RSVP at normal speed No loss, no gain

300 wpm

Try 300, then 700: at 700 you see every word but keep less.

How it works: one word at a time at a fixed point; no eye jumps, but also no going back and no preview, and the machine sets the pace. Spritz (2014) made it popular with the red pivot letter our app copies.

  • Blocking the jump back hurts comprehension. In normal reading of single sentences, text already read was masked so rereading found nothing; comprehension fell (the reported drop, from about 75% to about 50% correct, is not checked). Schotter, Tran & Rayner 2014. This was not RSVP, and whether ← (a jump to the sentence start) restores the benefit is my inference.
  • Spritz against normal reading at 250 wpm: literal comprehension worse, inferential the same, more eye fatigue from fewer blinks. Benedetto et al. 2015 (speed as reported by Di Nocera et al. 2018; n not verified)
  • Where the losses start: Ricciardi & Di Nocera 2017 tested 250 and 450 wpm: no loss at 250, a loss at 450. Di Nocera et al. 2018 (PDF), 209 people, 250–450 wpm against normal reading: with the stricter corrected test, no loss up to 350 and losses at 400 and 450; with simple pairwise tests, normal reading already won at 300 and 350. Scores fell steadily with speed.
  • Those speeds are settings, not real rates: Spritz pauses longer on some words, so its 300 setting really ran at 230 wpm, 350 at 265 and 400 at 305 (Di Nocera 2018, Table 1). None of these studies let readers go back.
  • Normal static text beat RSVP at 700 and 1,000 wpm. Acklin & Papesh 2017 (n not verified)
  • RSVP with no pause between sentences did worse than skimming for the same time. Masson 1983, via Rayner et al. 2016
Pros
About matches normal reading at normal speeds; no eye travel; works in a small window.
Cons
Not faster at equal comprehension. Small losses can start from a real rate of about 230 wpm, literal detail first. More eye fatigue.

In our app: ← returns to the sentence start, a deliberate jump back. Like Spritz, the app holds some words longer: clause ends 1.5×, sentence ends 2×, paragraph ends 2.5×, words over 8 letters 1.3× (Reading.swift). On about 980 words of prose that makes the 400 setting a real 357 wpm, and 300 a real 268; see decision D1.

4 Phrase and cascade layouts Helps some

cascade: indented by grammar
phrase RSVP 300 wpm

How it works: text is broken at phrase boundaries, and in a cascade indented by sentence structure, so the layout shows how the sentence is built.

  • Large randomised trial, school-age: 4,266 students in grades 7–8 reading in cascade format under an hour a week showed tiny but significant gains in overall English and writing scores (effect sizes 0.05 and 0.07); the reading-comprehension subtest showed no clear difference. Speed was not measured. Tate et al. 2019, sample from the What Works Clearinghouse
  • Adult studies so far are by the format's developers: Live Ink (Walker et al. 2005) and Cascade Reading's 2025 preprints (site); treat them as vendor claims.
  • Phrase RSVP against single-word RSVP: no head-to-head study found. One 1984 study of rapidly shown multi-word segments (Cocklin, Ward, Chen & Juola) may be one; I could not read it.
Pros
Gives back some preview and grouping that single-word RSVP removes. Cheap to add.
Cons
The trial evidence is on teenagers' overall English and writing scores, not comprehension or adult speed. Good phrase breaks need a parser; punctuation alone is rough.

In our app: phrase RSVP is a natural mode, and an open question in the literature; see decision D2.

5 Letter spacing and line length Helps some

extra letter spacing

How it works: wider spacing reduces crowding, where neighbouring letters interfere; line length changes how often the eye must jump to a new line.

  • Dyslexic children aged 8–14 read faster and more accurately with extra-wide letter spacing; typical readers did not speed up. Zorzi et al. 2012 (the figures quoted in the press, +20% speed and doubled accuracy, are not verified against the paper)
  • Long lines (100 characters) were read faster than short ones (25) with equal comprehension; about 55 characters was rated easiest. Dyson & Kipping 1998, Dyson & Haselgrove 2001 (figures from abstracts, not verified)
  • Dyslexia fonts: no gain from the letter shapes in controlled tests (Wery & Diliberto 2017); earlier gains came from their wider spacing.
Pros
Real help for dyslexic readers; costs nothing.
Cons
No speed gain for typical adult readers.

6 Skimming Trades comprehension

How it works: deliberately reading only parts of the text, chosen by your goal.

  • 2–4× faster with lower comprehension; fine for finding a fact or getting the gist of a familiar topic. Rayner et al. 2016
  • Readers trained in speed reading and readers trained to skim both reached about 600–700 wpm against 250 for normal readers; neither could answer detail questions about text they had not fixated. Just, Masson & Carpenter 1980, via Rayner et al. 2016
Pros
The honest fast mode: you choose what to lose.
Cons
Details are gone; not reading.

7 Fast RSVP, speed-reading courses, suppressing inner speech Trades or hurts

  • A speed-reading course raised speed from 280 to 400 wpm while comprehension fell from 81% to 74%. Calef et al. 1999, via Rayner et al. 2016
  • Training peripheral vision did not produce faster reading with good comprehension. Brim 1968; Sailor & Ball 1975, via Rayner et al. 2016
  • Saying irrelevant words aloud while reading, to block inner speech, hurt comprehension; finger tapping did not. Inner speech is part of understanding, not a habit to train away. Rayner et al. 2016
Pros
Faster on the clock.
Cons
Comprehension pays for it, every time it was measured.

8 Pacers, scrolling text, eye-tracking displays Untested

pacer 300 wpm
scrolling text 300 wpm

How they work: a pacer is a finger, pointer or highlight the eye follows; scrolling text moves the line past a fixed window; eye-tracking displays react to where you look.

  • Pacers: no controlled study found; the review mentions them only anecdotally.
  • Scrolling text: no recent controlled study found beyond older RSVP work.
  • Eye-tracking highlighting: 70 second graders read faster with fewer regressions and the same comprehension, but preferred plain text (Ergonomics 2025); a 13-person pilot found no difference. Needs an eye tracker.

9 Colour gradients across lines (BeeLine Reader) Untested in adults

colour runs across each line and turns back on the next

How it works: the end of each line matches the start of the next in colour, to guide the eye back to the next line.

  • Dutch children: faster only for 2nd graders with long, tightly spaced lines; 3rd graders possibly understood less and found it harder to read. Koornneef & Kraal 2022
  • The "10–30% faster" figures are the vendor's; no adult controlled study found.
  • The return to the next line is not where reading time goes. Rayner et al. 2016

In our app: not applicable; RSVP has no lines.

10 Bold word starts (Bionic Reading) No effect

first half of each word in bold

How it works: bolding the start of each word is claimed to guide the eye and let the brain fill in the rest.

  • 53 adults, eye-tracked, two passages each way: 131.1 against 130.8 wpm (p = 0.9); fixations unchanged. Beelders 2025
  • 100 paragraphs, Bionic against normal: no difference in reading time. Snell 2024, "No, Bionic Reading does not work"
  • A self-selected online test, 1,916 readers analysed, with the order of fonts randomised: no speed gain. Readwise 2022
Reading to learn is a different question. For a textbook, the studies point away from speed altogether: the gains come from recalling, explaining and manipulating what you read. That review has its own page: Beyond reading.

4 · Decisions

Two cheap changes follow from the evidence; both wait on you

D1 · Default speed

Lower the default from 400 to 300 wpm?

This only matters for silent reading, and only until you first press ↑ or ↓: the app then remembers your speed, and in V mode the voice sets the pace. Our 400 setting reads at a real 357 wpm (method 3), above every rate where the studies found no loss; 300 reads at a real 268, close to Spritz's 350 setting (real 265), where the strict test found no loss and the simple one a small one. ↑ still goes faster whenever you want to skim.

Recommended300 wpmStarts new readers near the edge of where losses begin, not past it; a one-line change.
Keep 400 wpmYou have already set your own speed, so the default only affects a fresh install.

D2 · Phrase mode

Add a key that switches between one word and short phrases?

Phrase display gives back some grouping that single words remove (method 4), and I found no study comparing phrase RSVP with single-word RSVP, so your own use is the test. A first version would break at punctuation and keep short words with the next one; a grammar-aware version is a later step.

RecommendedBuild it as a toggleA small change with tests in ReaderCore, the part of the app that splits text into words and times them. Then a week of articles at the same speed, alternating modes, with three questions you write after each: keep phrases if you answer more, or prefer it at equal scores.
Not nowThe evidence for phrase layouts is weak: tiny gains in teenagers' writing scores, none clear in comprehension or speed.

5 · Plan

Once you pick, each change is small

  1. D1: one number in Reader.swift, the file that runs the reading panel.
  2. D2: one new function in ReaderCore that groups words into phrases, tests for where phrases break, and a key (P, currently unused) to switch.
  3. Keep V mode as is: listening is the best-supported fast option, and V is it.
Not verified, and gaps in the literature

  • The specific figures from Rayner et al. 2016 (250 ms, 10%, 30% skipped, Spritz's 80%, 2× speech ≈ 300 wpm, Calef, Just et al., Masson) are from the review as summarised; its full text was unreachable. Its overall conclusion was checked against the abstract.
  • Spritz as a company after 2014, and its speed claims, are not covered.
  • Zorzi 2012 effect sizes and sample counts; Dyson's line-length figures; Kuster's dyslexia-font study details.
  • Results of Öquist & Goldstein 2003 (adaptive RSVP, n = 16) not retrieved.
  • No controlled study found for: pacers; phrase against single-word RSVP; RSVP plus a synced voice; BeeLine with adults; AI summaries as a speed aid.
