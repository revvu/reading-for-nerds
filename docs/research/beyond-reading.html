Reading for Nerds · research · Nov 7 · companion to Reading methods

To learn from a passage, make yourself recall, explain or work with it: in the checked studies that gains two to three times more than swapping the text for video or VR

Effect size: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. Each bar is a different meta-analysis with its own comparison, so the ratio is loose; checked bars alone give 0.55–0.85 against 0.24–0.28, or 2.0–3.5×.

Problem: You asked how to turn a highlighted passage into understanding, and whether richer media make reading obsolete. Done is what studies measured, ranked for an app built on "highlight, press a key", with a demo of each, a test of Apple's free on-device model, and a sketch of the feature. 1 · The words this page uses

Feeling that you understood and having learned are different measurements, and they often disagree

Feels like learning

Rereading, a clear summary, a fluent AI explanation.
High comfort, weak on a test a week later.

Is learning

Recalling it, explaining it, predicting, building something with it.
More effort, stronger on a test a week later.

  • Effect size (g or d): the gain measured in standard deviations (SD) of the comparison group, so studies can be compared. 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large; 0.5 moves an average learner to about the 69th percentile.
  • Meta-analysis: a study that pools the results of many studies into one effect size. Randomised: people were assigned to groups by chance. Preregistered: the analysis plan was filed before the data came in. n: the number of people.
  • Delayed test: a test days later. Many techniques that lose on an immediate test win on a delayed one, and that is the test that matters.
  • Retrieval practice: answering questions from memory instead of rereading. Spacing: repeating that over days instead of all at once.
  • Self-explanation: explaining to yourself why each step or claim holds.
  • Fluency illusion: ease while studying mistaken for learning. The 2024–2025 studies of AI help below hit it repeatedly.
  • Expertise reversal: extra explanation that helps a beginner gets in an expert's way, so help should fade as you learn a topic.
  • Scaffolded: with guidance built in, such as hints, worked examples or prompts, which is taken away as you improve.

4 · Each idea, with a demo

Every demo uses the same passage about eye movements, so each one teaches you the same thing a different way

1 Questions now, asked again over days Strongest

1. Where in reading does most of the work happen?

2. Why do the eyes sometimes jump backwards?

How it works: pulling an idea out of memory strengthens it and shows you what you missed; spreading those pulls over days makes them last.

  • Rereading won on a test five minutes later; testing yourself won on the delayed tests, two days and a week later. Roediger & Karpicke 2006
  • Practice testing and spacing were the two techniques rated high utility in the major review of ten study techniques; rereading, summarising and highlighting (marking text to reread) were rated low. Dunlosky et al. 2013
  • Meta-analyses: g ≈ 0.61 over 272 effect sizes (Adesope et al. 2017), g = 0.50 (Rowland 2014). Both figures are from summaries, not checked against the papers.
  • Questions written by an LLM beat no retrieval practice in a classroom study of about 60 students; not randomised (fixed week order, different material each week). Preprint, 2025
Pros
The most replicated effect in the field. The model's easiest task: it wrote mostly usable questions for all five passages tried (section 6).
Cons
The benefit shows only days later, and it feels harder than rereading. Needs the app to remember passages and bring them back.

2 Explain it back to a tutor that asks, not tells Strong

In your own words: if the eyes spend so little time moving, what is the reader doing most of the time, and why go back?

Tick the ones your explanation covered. A tutor would ask you about the ones you missed, rather than tell you.

How it works: producing your own "why" builds connected understanding; a tutor that prompts instead of answering keeps you doing that work.

  • Self-explanation prompts: g = 0.55 over 69 effect sizes; the authors call for computer-generated prompts as the next step. Bisra et al. 2018
  • Human tutors d = 0.79; computer tutors that check each step of your work d = 0.76. VanLehn 2011
  • Harvard physics, n = 194, each student had both conditions: a GPT-4 tutor built on teaching best practice beat an active-learning class; the authors put the gain at 0.73–1.3 SD. No delayed test. How strictly the tutor held back answers is in a supplement I did not read. Kestin et al. 2025
  • About 1,000 Turkish high-school students: plain GPT-4 lifted practice scores 48% but the unaided exam fell 17% against control; a version with tutoring rules lifted practice 127% and left the exam even. Bastani et al. 2025
Pros
Tutoring is one of the largest effects in education, and a language model makes it available on any passage.
Cons
Only works if the tutor holds back answers, and the model must judge your explanation, a harder task than writing questions. Typing takes time.

3 Predict first, then see the explanation Promising

Guess first: if your eyes never had to move at all, how much faster could you read?

About 10%. A stop on a word lasts about 250 ms and a jump about 30 ms, so jumps are 30 / (250 + 30) ≈ 11% of the time. Figures from the review by Rayner et al. 2016, cited on the companion page. RSVP also shows the words your eyes would skip, so it gives some of that back.

How it works: committing to a guess makes you notice the gap the explanation fills. On a highlighted passage, the app would ask you to predict its claim or outcome from the first sentence.

  • Preregistered, n = 1,200, SAT-style maths: GPT-4 explanations beat answer-only, and helped most when people tried the problem first; even explanations with errors beat answer-only. Short-term test only. Kumar, Rothschild, Goldstein & Hofman (not checked against the paper; the page was unreachable)
  • Trying a problem before being taught ("productive failure") beat teaching first: 53 studies, g = 0.36. Sinha & Kapur 2021

4 Build a concept map Strong

Click two ideas to link them.

How it works: deciding how ideas connect forces you to work out the structure, which a list of facts hides.

  • Concept maps and knowledge retention: 55 studies, n = 5,818. Nesbit & Adesope 2006
  • 142 effect sizes, n = 11,814: concept maps g = 0.58 overall; building one g = 0.72, studying a ready-made one g = 0.43. Schroeder et al. 2018 (figures from summaries; the paper was unreachable)
Pros
The app could suggest the ideas and leave the linking to you.
Cons
Slow for a single paragraph; better for a chapter. Handing you a finished map loses much of the gain.

5 An example from your own field Helps beginners

Hand-written for this demo; in the app the model would write it from what you tell it you know.

How it works: tying a new idea to something you already know lowers the load and raises interest.

  • 145 ninth-graders, randomised: algebra problems rewritten around each student's interests made them faster and more accurate, and the gain carried to later plain problems; students who were struggling gained most. Walkington 2013
  • Meta-analysis: retention g = 0.48, transfer g = 0.36, from only 6 effect sizes. Lin et al. 2024 (figures from summaries, not checked)
  • Limits: extra explanation that helps beginners hurts experts (Kalyuga 2007), and interesting but irrelevant detail reduces learning (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020).

A simplified model built from the review's averages, not a study. RSVP sets jumps, skipping and jumping back all to 0: try that, then just the jumps.

How it works: changing one cause and watching the effect builds intuition that prose describes but does not give.

  • Simulations in higher education, 145 studies: g = 0.85, larger with scaffolding; beginners gained from worked examples, advanced learners from reflection. Chernikova et al. 2020
  • Students who built circuits in a computer simulation instead of with real kit did better on a conceptual test, and then on building a real circuit. Finkelstein et al. 2005
  • "Explorable explanations" (Bret Victor, 2011) and Distill's interactive-article survey (Hohman et al. 2020) are an essay and a design survey, not controlled studies.
  • Models generated from a passage by a language model: no controlled learning study found.

7 A step diagram or animation, for processes only Small, except procedures

Theyjumpfromwordtoword.

Press Next step to walk through one cycle of reading.

How it works: showing change as change, rather than describing it.

  • 26 studies: animation beat static pictures, d = 0.37; for physical procedures d = 1.06; decorative animation added nothing. Höffler & Leutner 2007 (figures from summaries, not checked)
  • 61 studies, n = 7,036: g = 0.23 overall. Berney & Bétrancourt 2016 (not checked)
  • Many animation benefits vanish when the static version carries the same information; animations are often too fast to follow. Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt 2002 (not checked)

Why Next step comes first: letting you set the pace of each segment is one of Mayer's design rules for multimedia learning ("segmenting").

8 A one-click summary or simplification Avoid as a default

How it works: the model does the reading for you, which is exactly the effort that produces learning.

  • Preregistered, n = 405, ages 14–15, tested three days later: studying with an LLM alone did worse on comprehension and retention than taking notes, or notes plus the LLM; students preferred the LLM. Kreijkes et al. 2025
  • n = 91 university students, randomised: ChatGPT lowered effort against web search, and their written reasoning was weaker. Stadler, Bannert & Sailer 2024
  • Summarising is rated low utility as a study technique. Dunlosky et al. 2013
  • Meta-analyses claiming "ChatGPT improves learning" are unreliable: at least one has been retracted (from the research summary; citation not checked here).

5 · Is reading obsolete?

Swapping text for another medium gains a little; making the reader act gains more

What text does well

  • You set the pace, jump anywhere and look back.
  • Dense, precise, searchable.
  • Beats listening on inference questions (section 2).

What text does badly

  • Change over time: animation helps, most for physical procedures.
  • Spatial and causal structure: diagrams and maps help.
  • Intuition for a system: scaffolded simulations help.
MediumCompared withEffectSource
Videoother teaching (swapped in)0.28; as an add-on, 0.80Noetel et al. 2021, 105 randomised trials
VRnon-immersive teaching0.24; one study found more presence and less learningWu, Yu & Gu 2020; Makransky et al. 2019
Animationstatic pictures0.23–0.37 (not checked)Berney & Bétrancourt 2016; Höffler & Leutner 2007
Gamesnon-game teaching0.33Clark et al. 2016, 57 comparisons

None of these compares the medium with a reader who works with the text, which is where the larger effects in section 3 sit. So the claim the studies support is narrower than "reading is obsolete": reading passively is a weak way to learn, and the bigger upgrade is to make the reader produce something. The medium matters for content about change, space or systems, and there too the best formats ask you to act.

9 · Plan

If yes and questions: build it, then test it against plain reading on your own passages

  1. The learning key, the question panel and the local store, shown only on Macs where the model is available.
  2. Reviews after 1, 3 and 7 days from the menu-bar icon.
  3. The test: alternate passages, so half get questions and half are only read once in the reader. On day 7 the app quizzes you on both halves with the same kind of question.
  4. After about 20 passages, keep it if the question half is clearly ahead; drop it if they are level. One reader is a noisy test, so a small gap means nothing.
Not verified, and gaps in the literature
  • Figures from summaries, not checked against the papers: Adesope and Rowland; Schroeder; Lin 2024; Höffler & Leutner, Berney & Bétrancourt, Tversky; Kumar et al.
  • The exact questions from the first model run were not saved, so the fact judge could not check them; its reruns gave usable questions.
  • Which Macs support Apple Intelligence was not checked here; only this one was tested.
  • Google's "Learn Your Way" (n = 60, a preprint by Google) turns a chapter into personalised text, maps, audio and quizzes and beat a PDF reader, but every participant used the quizzes, so what caused the gain is unknown.
  • No controlled study found for: LLM-written self-explanation prompts on prose; LLM-generated diagrams, animations or simulations; RSVP for study.
